How to use your iPhone as a teleprompter, without looking like you’re reading

A teleprompter scrolls your script while you talk to the camera, so you never lose your place and never look down. Your iPhone can be one. Here is how to set it up, and the few things that decide whether you look like you’re talking or reading.

Read a script to camera

Reading a script to camera from memory takes rehearsal, and reading it off a sheet of paper or a laptop beside the lens shows: your eyes keep leaving the viewer. A teleprompter puts the words where you’re already looking and moves them up as you talk.

Scenarist has one built in. It scrolls the script you wrote, at the pace you set, in type as big as you want. This lesson reads the YouTube script from lesson 4, Why Every Screenplay Is in Courier, on an iPhone.

Action

Open the teleprompter

Open the script, then:

  1. Tap the ••• menu.
  2. Tap Analyze, then Teleprompter.

The teleprompter fills the screen with the lines you say, and only those: in a YouTube script the hook, the content and the call to action; in a screenplay, everything on the page. B-roll notes, on-screen text, ad breaks and timestamps stay in the script, where they belong, and out of your way.

For a video script, the editor also tells you how long it will run, from the words you’ll say, at about 150 words a minute. Lesson 4’s script reads ~0:36: the B-roll and on-screen text play under the voice, so they don’t count.

The teleprompter is part of Scenarist Pro.

Action

Play, pause, go back

Press play. You get three seconds to look into the lens, counted down in red, yellow and green, and then the script starts to move. From then on:

  • Tap anywhere on the script to pause, and again to go on.
  • Drag down to go back a line or two if you stumble, or up to skip ahead. Dragging pauses it; tap to go on.
  • The round arrow left of play takes you back to the top and sets the clock to zero. Press play, and the countdown runs again.

The screen stays awake while the prompter is open, so it won’t lock in the middle of a take.

Action

Your speed, your pace

Set the speed to how you talk, not the other way round. Read the first few lines out loud with it running:

  • If the words run ahead of you, slide toward the tortoise.
  • If you’re waiting for them, slide toward the hare.

A prompter that’s too fast makes you rush; one that’s too slow makes you pause in odd places. When it’s right, you stop noticing it. The clock beside play shows how long you’ve been talking, which helps when a video has to come in under a length.

Action

Big text, still eyes

Make the text bigger than feels right. Big type means three or four words to a line, so your eyes barely move. Small type means long lines, and eyes sweeping from side to side are what give a read away.

Shot

Where the phone goes

This matters more than any setting. Scenarist scrolls the script; your camera, or a second phone, does the filming. Put the prompter right under the lens, as close to it as you can, and keep your eyes near the top of the screen, on the lines nearest the lens.

The closer the words are to the lens, the less your eyes leave it, and to the person watching, you’re looking at them. Sitting a little further back helps too: from further away, the same eye movement is a smaller angle.

On iPhone Duo, half-fold it and stand it on the desk: the script scrolls on the upright half, and the controls lie on the flat half, under your hand.

Action

Mirror and dark mode

A teleprompter rig puts a sheet of glass at an angle in front of the lens, with the phone lying under it; the camera films through the glass while you read the reflection. A reflection is back to front, so turn on mirror, the arrows at the top: the glass flips the words back the right way.

Keep it in dark mode. White words on black give off little light, so the screen won’t light up your face or shine in your glasses. The sun at the top switches to black on white if you ever need it.

Character

Read like you talk

Don’t read to the lens; talk to one person behind it. Run the script once out loud before you record. If a line trips you up every time, it’s the line, not you: fix it in the script, and it’s fixed in the prompter.
