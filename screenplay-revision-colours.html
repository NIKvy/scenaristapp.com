Screenplay revision colours, in order

Once a script goes into production it stops being a document and becomes a version-control system made of coloured paper. Here is the order, and what it is for.

Guide · 3 minute read

Scene heading

Why colours at all

On a shooting script, pages change constantly and dozens of people are holding copies. Reprinting the whole script every time is impossible, so only changed pages are reissued — on a different colour of paper.

A crew member flips through their script and sees at a glance which pages are current. The colour is the version number, and it is legible from across a room.

Action

The standard order

#ColourTypically
1WhiteProduction draft — the locked original
2BlueFirst revision
3PinkSecond revision
4YellowThird revision
5GreenFourth revision
6GoldenrodFifth revision
7BuffSixth revision
8SalmonSeventh revision
9CherryEighth revision

After cherry the cycle repeats as double white, double blue, and so on. Most productions never get that far. Some do.

Character

Locked pages and asterisks

When a script is locked, page numbers stop moving. If a revision adds half a page between 42 and 43, the new page becomes 42A rather than pushing everything after it down by one. Schedules, call sheets, and breakdowns all reference page numbers, so renumbering would break the production.

Changed lines are marked with an asterisk in the right margin, and the revision colour and date appear in the header. So a page tells you three things before you read a word: which pass it belongs to, when it was issued, and exactly which lines moved.

Action

Does this apply to you

If you are writing a spec script, no. Send white pages. Revision colours on an unproduced screenplay read as costume rather than craft.

It starts mattering the moment something is being made — even a short with six people. The instant more than one person is holding a printed page, you need a way to know whose copy is current.
