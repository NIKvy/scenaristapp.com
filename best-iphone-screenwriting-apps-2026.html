What actually matters on a phone

Formatting without a keyboard

On a desktop, Tab and Enter cycle through element types and every screenwriter's hands know the pattern. On a phone there is no Tab key. An app either solves this properly — with a persistent element bar, smart detection, and autocomplete that learns your locations and characters — or it makes you fight the formatting instead of writing. The other answer is to skip the keyboard entirely: dictation that understands spoken element commands sidesteps the problem rather than working around it.

Whether your work survives

The single loudest complaint across every screenwriting app's reviews, for years, is lost work. Broken Dropbox sync. Cloud saves that silently fail. A crash that takes the last hour with it. Before you commit a project, find out where the file actually lives and what happens when the network drops mid-sentence.

What comes back out

PDF is enough for contests and most reads. The moment a producer or a development exec is involved, they will ask for .fdx. Fountain matters if you want plain text you can open in anything in ten years. An app that only exports PDF is a dead end — a flat PDF throws away the scene structure underneath.

The apps, individually

Final Draft

Still the industry default, and that is a real advantage rather than inertia alone — when someone asks for "a Final Draft file," this is what they mean. But it is a $249 desktop purchase and the mobile app is a companion to it. If your scripts go to studios and you already own it, stay. If you are starting out, $249 to write your first short is hard to justify.

WriterDuet

The best real-time collaboration in the category, genuinely. Three scripts free, then $89/year. Cloud-native, which means excellent sync and a hard dependency on connectivity. If you write with a partner, this is your answer and nothing here will change that.

Fade In

$79.95, once, for a professional desktop feature set — the best value on this list if you want to own your software. The mobile app is a companion rather than a primary writing environment.

Celtx

Not really a screenwriting app so much as a pre-production suite with a script editor attached. If you are producing your own work and need breakdowns, schedules, and call sheets in the same place, the $14.99/month makes sense. If you just want to write, you are paying for a lot you will never open.

Arc Studio

A modern cloud editor with strong outlining tools and a usable free tier. Web, desktop, and iOS. Good middle ground if you plan structurally, though the free plan caps how many scripts you can keep.

Highland 2

Beloved by Fountain writers and made by working screenwriters. It is macOS only, so on the question this article is asking, it is out.

Scenarist

Mine. Built for iPhone from the start rather than scaled down from a desktop product, which shows up in small places: a persistent element bar instead of a Tab key, autocomplete that fills in scene headings from locations you have already used, and an editor that treats each element as its own row rather than one long text field.

The things most apps charge for are free here — unlimited scripts, smart formatting, autocomplete, PDF and Fountain export, iCloud sync, find and replace, focus mode, notes and bookmarks, and 30-day recovery for anything you delete. Pro adds .fdx export, revision tracking in the industry colour sequence, index cards, script statistics, character reports, a full-screen teleprompter with mirror mode, and print-ready preview.

Voice dictation runs entirely on-device. Free covers per-element dictation — say "interior coffee shop day" and you get INT. COFFEE SHOP - DAY. Pro adds Voice to Script: spoken commands — "scene heading", "character", "dialogue", "cut to" — switch element type as you talk, so a whole scene builds itself in correct format without you touching the keyboard. It works on a plane.

There is no account. Your scripts live on your device and sync through your own iCloud if you want them to. Nothing reaches a server I control, because there isn't one.

Where it falls short, honestly: no real-time collaboration, no desktop app, and it is new — a fraction of the track record Final Draft has. If you need a co-writer in the document with you today, use WriterDuet.

Picking one

  • Your scripts go to studios or agencies. Final Draft. The compatibility is the product.
  • You write with a partner. WriterDuet.
  • You want to own it outright and work on a Mac or PC. Fade In at $79.95.
  • You are producing as well as writing. Celtx.
  • You write on your phone and want the pro tools without a subscription. Scenarist.
  • You are on a Mac and love Fountain. Highland 2.

Whatever you pick, do one test before you commit real work: write two pages, export .fdx, and open it somewhere else. Round-tripping catches the problems that only surface at the worst moment.

Common questions

Can you really write a whole screenplay on an iPhone?

Yes. The formatting problem is solved. What is left is comfort — most people writing feature-length work still want a keyboard eventually. But a first draft, a short, a spec scene, an episode outline? Entirely doable on a phone, and far more of it gets written because the phone is already in your pocket.

Which apps export .fdx?

Final Draft, WriterDuet, Fade In, Arc Studio, Celtx, and Scenarist. Some gate it behind a paid tier, Scenarist included.

Is there anything without a subscription?

Fade In and Highland sell one-time desktop licences. On iPhone specifically, Scenarist offers a one-time Lifetime unlock alongside its monthly and annual plans.

What is Fountain, and should I care?

A plain-text screenplay format — you write INT. KITCHEN - DAY in a text file and any Fountain-aware app renders it correctly. It matters because it is the one format guaranteed to still open in twenty years, whatever happens to the company that made your software.
